Advice to Youth
Mark Twain — 1882
"Truth is mighty and will prevail" — the most majestic compound fracture of fact which any of woman born has yet achieved. For the history of our race, and each individual's experience, are sewn thick with evidences that a truth is not hard to kill, and that a lie well told is immortal.
— Mark Twain, Advice to Youth (1882)

Twain delivered this as a graduation address, the genre practically daring him to be sincere, and he spent it instead building a case for lying as a craft that rewards patience and diligence. The line about the Boston monument comes a few sentences later: a memorial built for the wrong man, the actual discoverer forgotten, the error too well established to correct. He never raises his voice about it. The joke works because the sentence structure is doing the same thing a good lie does — sounding entirely reasonable until you check it against the facts.
