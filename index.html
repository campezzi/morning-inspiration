Walden, "The Ponds"
Henry David Thoreau — 1854
A lake is the landscape’s most beautiful and expressive feature. It is earth’s eye; looking into which the beholder measures the depth of his own nature. The fluviatile trees next the shore are the slender eyelashes which fringe it, and the wooded hills and cliffs around are its overhanging brows.
— Henry David Thoreau, Walden (1854)

Thoreau spends the whole chapter measuring Walden Pond, sounding it through the ice with a line and a stone, and then, in the middle of all that surveying, he gives the water a face. The trees along the shore become eyelashes and the cliffs become brows, and he lets the figure run a little further than is strictly sensible.

The odd turn is in the middle: the beholder looks in and takes a reading of himself. The pond is the instrument, and he is what gets measured. It is a quiet sentence, and it asks nothing of you but to stand at the edge for a while.
